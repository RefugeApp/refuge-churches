Marriage & Temptation
My wife hasn't wanted to be intimate with me in two years. I've tried everything. I'm starting to feel things for someone at work and I hate myself for it.
R

The ache you're describing is real and heavy. Two years without intimacy in a marriage creates a particular kind of loneliness, and the fact that feelings have begun to surface toward someone else has triggered the self-loathing. That reaction itself tells you something important about where your conscience still stands.

The temptation is to treat the new feelings as the problem that needs solving. They are not. They are a signal that something in the marriage has gone unattended for a long time. Feelings arise; what matters is whether you feed them or bring the full weight of the marriage back into the light.

You said you've tried everything. What does that actually look like when you describe it plainly, without softening the details? And what has her response been, not in words but in the pattern that has held for two years?

The work colleague is not an answer. She is a doorway out of the marriage's unresolved pain. Walking through it will not relieve the self-hatred; it will compound it.

God is not disgusted by the struggle. He sees the full picture of what has gone unspoken between you and your wife. The next faithful step is not managing the feelings at work but returning, with whatever honesty you can muster, to the covenant you already made. That is where the real work waits.

Abuse & Forgiveness
My father abused me for years. He's dying and my family is pressuring me to reconcile. I'm a Christian. I know what I'm supposed to say. But I can't make myself mean it.
R

The abuse was real. Years of it do not dissolve because someone is dying or because your family needs the story to end neatly. That pressure to reconcile, to produce the words they want to hear, lands on the wound like another demand. You know the Christian language — forgive, honor your father, be reconciled — and you cannot force your soul to match the script. That is not rebellion. That is integrity refusing to lie.

Forgiveness in Scripture is not the same as reconciliation. One is a matter of the heart before God; the other requires safety, repentance, and the possibility of restored trust. Your father's approaching death does not rewrite what happened. The ache you feel is not unchristian. It is the body remembering what the mind has tried to organize into doctrine.

You say you cannot make yourself mean it. That refusal may be the most honest thing you have offered anyone in this situation. God does not traffic in performances.

What would it cost you to say the words anyway, just to quiet the family? And what would it cost you to stay silent and let the silence speak for itself? Those are two different prices. You do not have to decide tonight which one you can carry.

Prodigal Children
My son walked away from the faith two years ago and now he's living with his girlfriend. Every family dinner is a landmine. I'm losing him and I don't know how to hold on without pushing him further away.
R

This tension you describe — the fear of losing your son while every interaction feels like it could fracture further — is one of the oldest aches a parent carries. The desire to reach him collides with the reality that you cannot force his return, and forcing the issue risks exactly what you dread.

The prodigal son story does not begin with the father lecturing or strategizing. It begins with the father letting the son walk away with his inheritance, then watching the road every day without chasing him down. That waiting was not passivity; it was a form of love that refused to rewrite the truth to keep the boy close. Yet when the son finally turned, the father ran.

Your son's choices place him outside the pattern God designed for intimacy and commitment. That matters. At the same time, your relationship with him is not the same as endorsing those choices. Presence without pretense is costly on both sides.

What would it look like, in the next family dinner, to speak to the son you still have rather than the son you are trying not to lose?

Anxiety & Control
I can't shut my brain off at night. My chest goes tight and I run through everything that could go wrong. I've prayed about it more times than I can count, but the worry always comes back — and now I feel like my faith should be enough and it isn't.
R

Two things are happening at once, and it helps to pull them apart. There's the worry itself — the racing mind, the tight chest, the body bracing for a threat it can't quite name. And then there's a second weight stacked on top: the belief that a stronger faith would have shut this off by now, so the anxiety has quietly become evidence against you. That second layer is its own kind of suffering, and it isn't true.

Worry is rarely about the thing you're rehearsing at 2am. It's the mind trying to get out ahead of a future it cannot control — running every scenario as if certainty could be earned by enough vigilance. When Jesus told his followers not to be anxious, it wasn't a scolding for failing to relax. It was an invitation to set down a weight that was never yours to carry: tomorrow.

So look honestly at what the praying has been doing. "I've prayed about it more times than I can count" can be surrender — or it can be one more way of managing the outcome, bargaining for the certainty you're really after. From the outside those look identical. On the inside they feel nothing alike.

Here is the question worth sitting with: what is the specific thing you're afraid will happen if you stop bracing for it — and could you say that fear out loud, to God and to one person who can sit with you in it, instead of carrying it alone at 2am?

Honest answers

Before you trust something like this,
you should question it.

The questions pastors ask — about theology, privacy, safety, and the role Refuge should and should not play.

Is this just ChatGPT with Bible verses?

No. Generic AI is designed to be agreeable, not truthful. It collapses under pressure, defaults to "therapy-speak," and often drifts into theological compromise when pushed. Refuge is a closed-system architecture engineered for theological and pastoral rigor. It is built to hold the line on orthodox teaching and to navigate the complexities of the human soul without flinching. It doesn't just "know" Scripture; it is constrained by it.

What theological tradition does it follow?

Refuge is grounded in historic, orthodox Christianity. It is built to uphold the authority of Scripture and the classic tenets of the faith. It explicitly rejects progressive, revisionist, and culturally accommodating positions on the nature of God, the authority of the Word, human sexuality, and identity. Refuge is designed to support the shepherd — not to challenge his doctrine.

Does this replace me?

No. It shields you. Refuge handles the 2 AM triage so you don't have to. You get the "second session" version of your people — the ones who have already named their pain and are finally ready to be shepherded, rather than the ones in raw crisis who require four hours of your day just to stabilize.

Can I see what my members are talking about?

No. Refuge is architecturally designed to make member data inaccessible to church leadership. Privacy is not a secondary feature; it is the fundamental mechanism that enables honest reflection. If your people believe their private struggles might eventually land on your desk, they will not be honest. By guaranteeing absolute confidentiality, Refuge allows them to process the "first wave" of their crisis in private. This ensures that when they do seek your counsel, they arrive with the clarity and openness required for genuine shepherding.

What happens if someone is in crisis?

Refuge is equipped with professional-grade safety protocols that monitor for indicators of imminent danger or self-harm. If a conversation crosses that threshold, the system does not abandon the user. It immediately provides direct access to professional emergency resources while remaining fully present, continuing to offer stabilization and guidance. Refuge is designed to recognize its limits — it knows when to hand off to professionals — but it never leaves the user to face the crisis alone.

How do I introduce this to my congregation?

Lead by example. Spend five minutes with Refuge this week. Ask it a serious question you are actually carrying. Once you've experienced the clarity it provides, recommend it exactly as you would any other trusted resource: "I know some of you are struggling. I've found something to help you work through the problems you're carrying. It's private, built by a licensed counselor, and it's available the moment you need it."
